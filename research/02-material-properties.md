## 1. Summary

- **Timber (best evidenced).** Decay needs wood moisture content above about 20%. Spores germinate poorly below fibre saturation (about 25–30%), and active decay sits around 35–50% (NRC CBD-111). EN 350 heartwood classes from the sources found:
  - Western red cedar: class 2.
  - North American Douglas fir: class 3.
  - European larch: 3–4.
  - European oak: 2, with very wide variation.

  Sapwood is non-durable in every case. Weathering erodes softwood surfaces at roughly 33–101 µm per year (Williams et al., FPL), which is about 3–10 mm per century. Charring (shou sugi ban) did **not** consistently improve decay resistance in FPL soil-block tests. Thermal modification lowers equilibrium moisture content and slows early greying, but does not stop it.
- **Mineral renders and masonry.** Lime render is porous, takes up water and dries quickly. It is a sacrificial layer that is renewed periodically, and it is more bioreceptive than cement. Cement render absorbs little water but cracks and traps water behind it. Clay brick takes up water fast (sorptivity about 1.5 mm/min^½ for one common brick). Frost resistance in brick is governed by ASTM C216 grade SW (saturation coefficient ≤ 0.78 or the listed alternatives). For terracotta rainscreen, one product's data sheet gives 5–7% water absorption and a pass after 100 freeze–thaw cycles. Granite absorbs under 1%. Sandstone has 5–25% porosity and absorption of several percent up to about 10%.
- **Concrete.** Sorptivity is low (about 0.011 mm/s^½ for one ordinary Portland cement (OPC) mix). Fresh concrete has a high pH, which suppresses colonisation. As the surface carbonates, pH falls below about 10 and algae and biofilm can establish. Lower pH, roughness and porosity all raise bioreceptivity, but lab and field rankings disagree. Freeze–thaw durability depends on air entrainment and the CSA A23.1 F-class. The exact air percentages are not verified here.
- **Ground layer.** Clear gravel or drain rock works as a capillary break (capillary rise roughly 0.02–0.1 m, versus metres for silt). Topsoil water retention rises with organic matter. Raindrop splash can lift soil about 0.6 m. BC Building Code Part 9 uses 150 mm wood-to-ground clearance as the dampproofing trigger.
- **Damp-proof courses (DPCs).** The evidence is mainly standards scope and manufacturer data. One appraisal gives a 50-year life for a membrane, but only if it is protected from UV.
- **Innovative materials (low confidence).**
  - Hempcrete: excellent moisture buffering. Freeze–thaw sensitivity depends on the binder. It needs a breathable lime render, but a Swedish full-scale study found a cement render worked better in a cold, wet climate.
  - Bioreceptive concrete: demonstrated at lab scale (TU Delft, UPC).
  - Mycelium: water absorption is high and there is no outdoor service evidence.
  - Cork cladding: manufacturer claims only.
- **Biggest gaps.** Field drying rates for any material, real service-life and maintenance numbers, Vancouver-specific freeze–thaw exposure, and fibre-cement properties (the manufacturer PDFs could not be parsed).

## 5. Gaps

1. **Field drying rates.** No source gives drying times or rates for any edge material in a wet maritime climate. Drying levels (slow, moderate, fast) are currently inferred from porosity and vapour data. A WUFI or hygrothermal property database (Fraunhofer IBP, or NRC Canada material property sets) would be the right next source.
2. **Vancouver-specific exposure.** Freeze–thaw cycle counts, the wind-driven rain index and moss/algae growth rates for Vancouver were not researched in this layer. They are needed to translate lab freeze–thaw results into "some freeze–thaw" risk.
3. **Service life and maintenance cycles.** Only proxies were found: warranties, Australian durability classes, vendor anecdotes and a BRANZ membrane appraisal. Candidate sources include the BC Housing / HPO maintenance guides, BRE digests and the RICS/BCIS life expectancy tables.
4. **Bioreceptivity of timber, terracotta, fibre cement and granite** (moss, algae and lichen on cladding). Not covered; the bioreceptivity literature found is stone- and cementitious-focused.
5. **Thermal behaviour relevant to drying.** Only one coating-colour study (spruce, Norway) and a supplier light reflectance value (LRV) guide (South Pacific Timber NZ) were found. No thermal-mass and drying evidence was found.
6. **Clay plaster and cork in exterior wet climates.** The evidence is weak or manufacturer-only. Consider dropping exterior clay plaster from the Vancouver base zone, or allowing it only under a deep overhang, tagged illustrative.
7. **Primary PDFs unread.** Several key PDFs (FPL weathering, the woodpreservation.ca "Weathering of Wood" guide, the Amorim TDS, the BRANZ Hardie appraisal, Canada Brick) could not be text-extracted in this environment. Re-reading them would upgrade several [snippet] entries.
8. **EN 350 revision.** prEN 350 is in development, with a ballot listed for August 2026. The class values above may change.
