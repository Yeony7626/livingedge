# 04 — Ecology layer: evidence for The Living Edge (first region: Vancouver, BC)

Drafted 2026-10-12. Scope covers surface colonisation, bioreceptivity, the ground at the wall foot, decay organisms, drainage ageing, maintenance and wear, and materials designed with ecological intent.

Verification key (used in the `suggested confidence` column):
- **V-full**: the full text, or the relevant full section, was read.
- **V-abs**: only the abstract, an official summary or a publisher record was read.
- **V-snip**: only search-engine excerpts of the named source were seen. Treat these as provisional until the source is opened.

Confidence tags for the tool's rules:
- **qualitative-strong**: several sources agree, or one source is authoritative.
- **qualitative**: one credible source.
- **qualitative-weak**: secondary, lab-only or contested.
- **illustrative**: no direct evidence.

Rule text names no species and gives no rates.

## 1. Short summary

- **Moisture duration is the main driver.** How long a surface stays wet, after rain, splash, condensation or contact with damp ground, decides whether it gets colonised. Orientation, shade, nearby trees, overhangs and the splash zone at the base all act through this wetting and drying regime (ev.eco.05, .07, .08, .09).
- **The material sets the potential.** Guillitte (1995) called this bioreceptivity: the colonisation potential that comes from the material itself, separate from the environment's "accessibility" (ev.eco.01).
- **Main material levers:** surface roughness, open porosity and capillary water uptake, mineralogy and chemistry (including pH), and surface geometry at the cm to m scale, such as ledges and horizontal or diagonal faces versus vertical faces (ev.eco.03, .10, .11, .13, .14).
- **Bioreceptivity changes over time.** Weathering raises it ("secondary"). Cleaning changes it ("tertiary"), and recolonisation after cleaning tends to be faster than the first colonisation. Coatings and consolidants that are added can create new habitat ("quaternary") (ev.eco.02, .16).
- **Succession order is well supported; field timescales are poorly quantified.**
  - Order: phototrophic micro-organisms ("greening") come first; mosses, lichens and plants follow later (ev.eco.04, .06).
  - Lab tests compress this to weeks or months under nutrient sprinkling.
  - In the field, one three-site outdoor study saw no visible colonisation of new cementitious specimens during its exposure period (ev.eco.10).
  - Lichen arrival on new stone shows a lag of several years to nearly two decades, and arrival is erratic (ev.eco.15).
- **Fresh cementitious alkalinity as a delay is plausible but contested.** A low-pH binder fouled faster in the lab. A later study found that pH below 10 was not necessary for growth (ev.eco.10, .11). The tool should treat "fresh alkaline surface delays growth" as qualitative-weak.
- **At the wall foot:**
  - Trees cause foundation movement mainly by drying shrinkable clay soils. Setbacks are therefore soil-dependent: strict on clays (NHBC 4.2; Ottawa's marine-clay guidelines), much less critical on sands and gravels (ev.eco.26, .27).
  - Roots exploit existing defects in old, jointed or cracked drains (ev.eco.25).
  - Local BC guidance keeps infiltration features about 3 m from the house (ev.eco.28).
  - Mulch and gravel keep the soil beneath them moist and should not touch siding (ev.eco.24).
- **Decay organisms:**
  - Wood decay needs sustained high moisture: wood is generally treated as safe at or below about 19–20% moisture content, and decay needs about 30% or more for extended periods. Ground contact brings both moisture and fungal inoculum (ev.eco.19, .20).
  - In BC, carpenter ants favour moist, decaying wood and enter via vegetation and wood touching the house or soil (ev.eco.22, .23).
  - Cladding clearance above finished ground exists in the code to limit splash and pooling (ev.eco.21).
- **Maintenance and wear:**
  - Cleaning resets the surface only temporarily, and biocides lose effect within months to a few years (ev.eco.07, .08).
  - Repeated contact strips cryptogam cover where feet and hands land. Most of the loss happens during the first uses, and recovery is slow (ev.eco.18).
- **Weakest evidence:**
  - Gravel and drain silting over time has no measured residential data.
  - Mycelium has no exterior evidence.
  - Hemp-lime has only lab evidence.
  - There is no Vancouver-specific field data on facade colonisation.

## 3. Proposed qualitative growth-stage model (ECOLOGY layer)

### 3.1 Stages (surface)

| stage | label | what the user sees | evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | clean | material colour as built | — |
| S1 | biofilm | faint dulling/darkening, mostly in wet streaks | ev.eco.06, .16 |
| S2 | algae ("greening") | green/grey-green film; patchy, heaviest on wet bands | ev.eco.04, .06, .07, .08 |
| S3 | moss | cushions in joints, ledges, drip lines, base splash band | ev.eco.04, .07 |
| S4 | lichen / plants | lichen patches on stable surfaces; plants and woody seedlings where soil or debris has collected in joints | ev.eco.15, .17 |

Stages accumulate as a **mosaic**. One facade can show S2 on wet bands and S1 elsewhere. The tool should model stage per zone, not per wall:
- base splash band
- joints and ledges
- open face
- under the overhang
- touched or seat zones

### 3.2 Drivers that move a zone forward

1. **Wetness duration (master driver)** (ev.eco.05, .07, .08, .09):
   - Higher with orientation away from sun (north and west in the northern hemisphere), shade from trees or neighbours, no overhang, the splash zone near the ground, ponding or irrigated beds at the wall foot, and soil or mulch against the wall (ev.eco.21, .24, .28).
   - Lower with overhangs, a clear gravel gap that drains, vertical fluting that sheds water, and sun exposure.
2. **Material receptivity** (ev.eco.01, .03, .10, .11, .13, .14):
   - Higher with roughness, open porosity and capillary uptake, exposed aggregate, relief or ledges that hold water, and weathered ("secondary") surfaces.
   - Lower with smooth, dense, low-absorption surfaces.
   - Fresh alkaline cement or lime surfaces may delay S2 slightly (qualitative-weak, contested).
3. **Deposits and nutrients** (ev.eco.05, .07, .17):
   - Canopy drip, leaf litter and soil splash speed the move from S2 to S3 and from S3 to S4. Moss and rooted plants need accumulated substrate in joints and on ledges.
4. **Time since last reset**: see 3.4.

### 3.3 Drivers that hold a zone back or reset it

- **Cleaning** (ev.eco.02, .07, .16): resets to S0 or S1. The surface then sits at "tertiary" receptivity, so regrowth starts faster than on the new material. Aggressive cleaning roughens the surface and raises receptivity further. Recolonisation goes first to a generic film, then back toward the previous mix.
- **Biocidal finishes and inhibitor runoff** (ev.eco.05, .07, .08): delay S2 only temporarily (months to a few years). Copper runoff below metal flashings suppresses growth locally.
- **Human contact and wear** (ev.eco.18): keeps touched and stepped zones at S0–S1. Most clearing happens early in use. If use stops, regrowth is slow.

### 3.4 Indicative timing for the tool's 5 / 15 / 30-year views (ILLUSTRATIVE)

These bands are anchored loosely on the evidence but are not measured for Vancouver. Tag them **illustrative** in the tool.

- **5 years:**
  - Wet, shaded zones and the base splash band: S1–S2, possibly S3 in joints and ledges.
  - Sunny, sheltered open faces: S0–S1.
  - Anchors: biocide protection lasting at most a few years (ev.eco.08); field specimens not visibly colonised over a study period (ev.eco.10).
- **15 years:**
  - Wet zones: S2–S3, with first lichen patches possible on stable mineral surfaces. Lichen lag is about 5–18 years and erratic (ev.eco.15).
  - Dry zones: S1–S2.
- **30 years:**
  - Wet zones: S3–S4 mosaic, with plants where joints have filled.
  - Dry zones: S2, with lichen patches.
  - Touched zones stay lower throughout (ev.eco.18).

### 3.5 Ground at the wall foot (parallel qualitative state)

- **Base wetness** (low / moderate / high):
  - Raised by mulch or gravel against the wall, irrigated or infiltrating beds within about 3 m, soil above the cladding clearance, and blocked surface drainage (ev.eco.21, .24, .28).
  - Lowered by grade falling away from the wall, a clear gap, and overhangs.
- **Root influence** (low / moderate / high):
  - **Shrinkable clay:** scales with the plant's mature size and water demand relative to distance from the wall (ev.eco.26, .27).
  - **Non-shrinkable soils:** low for foundation movement.
  - **Drains:** risk where drains are old, jointed or cracked, regardless of soil (ev.eco.25).
- **Timber decay risk** (none / incipient / active), applies only to timber elements:
  - Requires sustained high moisture (ev.eco.19, .20). Ground contact and splash-zone timber can reach "active" within years.
  - Insect pressure follows the decay state and contact with plants or soil (ev.eco.22, .23).
- **Drain/gravel condition** (clear / silting / impaired): **illustrative only** (see Gaps).

## 4. Unverified and Gaps

### Unverified (seen only second-hand, or believed but not confirmed; do not use as rule evidence yet)

- **Carbonation thresholds.** A review on concrete biofouling, seen only as a search excerpt from a HAL (École des Mines de Saint-Étienne) document, reportedly says fresh concrete at pH about 11–13 resists colonisation and fouling begins near pH 9–10 after carbonation. The full citation was not obtained (HAL blocked access).
- **pH counter-evidence.** A field experiment on UK and Singapore seawalls, titled "Little evidence that lowering the pH of concrete supports greater…" (Macquarie University record), reportedly found no significant pH effect. It is marine, and only the title and search excerpt were seen.
- **Guillitte's original paper.** The volume and pages of Guillitte (1995), *Sci. Total Environ.* 167, 215–220, come from secondary listings. The exact title appears in two forms ("…building ecology studies" vs "…building materials ecology studies").
- **Armstrong lichenometry review.** The journal and year were not confirmed; only the Aston accepted manuscript was read. The Innes (1983) "8-year minimum" figure was seen only in a search summary; the review text reads "a minimum period of exposure".
- **Mycelium review authors.** The 2020 *Materials & Design* critical review is believed to be by Jones, M., Mautner, A., Luenco, S., Bismarck, A., John, S. Not confirmed.
- **Fraunhofer/Hochschule Coburg study.** One year of outdoor exposure reportedly found orientation (north/west vs south/east) significantly changed microbial community composition on facades. Only a search excerpt was seen; Fraunhofer's repository blocked access.
- **Krus & Sedlbauer (2006) and related Fraunhofer papers.** These reportedly cover night-time radiative cooling and condensation as the driver of algae on insulated renders. Seen only in search excerpts.
- **Mineral vs organic renders.** A Czech fluorometry/WUFI study reportedly found higher algal resistance on mineral than organic render systems. Only a search excerpt was seen.
- **NBC structural-wood clearance.** NBC 9.3.2.9 reportedly requires 450 mm clearance for structural wood above ground, or termite measures, where termites are known. Seen via a BC Building Code Appeal Board decision excerpt; it is relevant only where termites are present.
- **Termites in BC.** Native subterranean and dampwood termites are reportedly present on BC's south coast. A Canadian Forestry Service bulletin and the CWC page were seen only as excerpts (CWC returned 403). Sources disagree on how much dampwood termites matter in buildings.
- **Rain garden setbacks elsewhere.** Metro Vancouver (GVRD 2005) reportedly put rain gardens at least 3 m downslope of foundations that have footing drains. Seen only on a poster excerpt.
- **Permeable pavement clogging.** Field and lab studies reportedly found:
  - pedestrian permeable pavements kept high infiltration while vehicular ones clogged severely;
  - vacuum cleaning restores much of the lost infiltration;
  - a University of Toronto study showed maintenance can restore post-construction infiltration.
  Authors and journals were not confirmed.
- **Planting setback rules of thumb.** Extension-style rules (e.g. shrubs at least half their mature spread from the wall; evergreens at least 10 ft) come from non-authoritative or unconfirmed pages and are not used.
- **Roof moss.** Claims that moss forms mats more readily on timber shingles than on tiles, and that it is not normally harmful to tiles, come only from manufacturer and secondary pages.

### Gaps

1. **No Vancouver or Pacific Northwest field data on facade colonisation.** No source quantifies how fast renders, concrete, brick or timber at grade green up in Vancouver's wet-winter climate. All timing bands are illustrative. Suggest searching UBC, BCIT Building Science Centre of Excellence and RDH Building Science field reports.
2. **Field timescales generally.** Only the lichen lag (ev.eco.15) and biocide durability (ev.eco.07, .08) give real-world time anchors. The Manso et al. (2015) exposure duration was not found in the abstract and needs the full text.
3. **North-face effect.** It is supported qualitatively (ev.eco.08, .09; Sanmartín on aspect). No measured orientation comparison of coverage on facades was read in full.
4. **Drainage gravel ageing and silting.** No measured data were found for residential gravel strips or perforated foundation drains. Clogging mechanisms (fines migration, organic debris, roots at joints) are plausible and partly supported (ev.eco.25), but any rule here is **illustrative**. Permeable-pavement literature is the closest proxy (see Unverified).
5. **Vancouver soils.** No source was found describing whether typical Vancouver building soils are shrinkable. Clay-specific setback rules (ev.eco.26, .27) should apply only when the user marks the soil as clay, and should be tagged as UK/Ottawa-derived.
6. **Planting strips and wall-base moisture.** No measured study was found on how soil moisture at the wall foot varies with planting type, mulch or gravel. The rules rest on extension and municipal guidance (ev.eco.24, .28).
7. **Wear from sitting or touching building materials.** The only evidence comes from climbing on natural rock (ev.eco.18). No study of seats, handrails or plinths was found.
8. **Bioreceptive panels and green walls at the base.** Evidence is limited to greenhouse and one-year outdoor prototypes (ev.eco.13, .14). Nothing covers splash and soil contact at the base, or performance at 15 or 30 years.
9. **Hemp-lime and mycelium in the exterior splash zone.** No field evidence (ev.eco.12, .29, .30).
10. **Carpenter ant thresholds.** Moisture thresholds conflict (ev.eco.23). No PNW-specific quantitative data were found.
11. **Historic Environment Scotland.** The INFORM guide "Biological growth on masonry" (attributed in a secondary bibliography to Eklund & Young, 2013) and TAN 10 were located only as catalogue listings. Their text was not read; they are worth obtaining.
12. **NHBC 4.2 full text.** It is behind a login, so its water-demand categories and distance tables were not verified.
